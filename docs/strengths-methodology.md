# Strengths Profile — Methodology

Version 2.0 · 20 themes · 4 domains · 130 items

*An original forced-choice strengths instrument for practice, coaching and
assessment-centre use. Inspired by the strengths-assessment format but not
affiliated with, and not reproducing any content from, Gallup's
CliftonStrengths®.*

---

## 1. Purpose and positioning

This is a **development and practice instrument**, not a validated selection
test. It produces an *intra-individual* profile — a rank order of a person's own
strengths — to support conversation, coaching and self-reflection. It is
deliberately **not normed**, so it does not place a person against a population
and should not be used to screen or rank candidates against one another (§8).

## 2. Construct model — themes and domains

Twenty themes, five in each of four domains. The four-domain taxonomy is the
same widely used descriptive structure popularised by CliftonStrengths; the
theme names and all statement content are original.

| Domain | Themes |
|---|---|
| **Executing** — how you make things happen | Driver, Organiser, Finisher, Mender, Compass |
| **Influencing** — how you reach others | Persuader, Catalyst, Voice, Contender, Opener |
| **Relationship Building** — how you build bonds | Connector, Harmoniser, Empathiser, Cultivator, Welcomer |
| **Strategic Thinking** — how you take in the world | Analyst, Visionary, Learner, Originator, Navigator |

The eight themes added in v2 cover territory the original twelve did not reach:

| Theme | Territory |
|---|---|
| Mender | Drawn to what is broken; diagnosis and repair as their own reward |
| Compass | Work anchored to fixed principles; purpose as a precondition for effort |
| Contender | Motivated by comparison; performance measured against others |
| Opener | Turning strangers into contacts; initiating rather than maintaining |
| Cultivator | Energised by other people's growth; spotting and investing in potential |
| Welcomer | Noticing who is on the edge of a group and widening the circle |
| Originator | Generating options for their own sake; connecting the unrelated |
| Navigator | Seeing the route through complexity; anticipating where a plan breaks |

Five themes per domain keeps **domain share** (§5) comparable across domains. An
uneven split would make a domain's share partly an artefact of how many themes
it contains.

## 3. Item format — forced-choice, timed

Each item presents **two self-descriptor statements** and asks which is more like
the respondent, and how strongly, on a **5-point graded scale** with a neutral
midpoint. This is an **ipsative forced-choice** format: because one statement
must be traded off against another, the respondent cannot rate everything
highly, which controls **social-desirability** and **acquiescence** bias far
better than a Likert scale.

Each item carries a **20-second limit**. On timeout the response is recorded as
neutral. The limit captures a **spontaneous, top-of-mind reaction** rather than a
considered, self-curated one.

## 4. Item bank and balancing

- **130 items**, from a bank of **13 statements per theme** — 10 positively
  keyed, 3 reverse keyed.
- Generated by a **round-robin (circle-method) schedule**: 20 themes produce 10
  pairings per round, and 13 rounds give 130 items in which **every theme
  appears exactly 13 times** and **no theme is ever paired against itself**.
- Because appearances equal bank size, **every statement is shown exactly once**.
  No repeats within a sitting.
- Item order and left/right position are randomised per session.

Balanced exposure is a requirement for fair ipsative scoring — a theme appearing
more often would accumulate more opportunities to score.

**On length.** At 13 appearances each theme rests on fewer comparisons than the
17 used by the 12-theme v1, so themes are measured slightly less precisely. That
is the cost of breadth, not a defect: the same respondent effort now covers 20
themes instead of 12. Going to 17 appearances would mean 170 items and roughly
20 minutes under timer, where fatigue plausibly degrades data quality more than
the extra comparisons improve it.

## 5. Scoring

For each answered item the 5 scale positions allocate a fixed **2 points**
between the two statements:

| Position | → statement A | → statement B |
|---|---|---|
| Most like A | 2.0 | 0.0 |
| Slightly A | 1.5 | 0.5 |
| Neutral | 1.0 | 1.0 |
| Slightly B | 0.5 | 1.5 |
| Most like B | 0.0 | 2.0 |

A statement's allocation is **added** to its theme if positively keyed and
**subtracted** if reverse keyed. Theme totals are summed across the session, then
**min–max normalised to a 0–100 index** for display and ranked.

**Output**
- A **full rank order** of all 20 themes (0–100 index).
- The **top 5** as **signature strengths**.
- A **domain balance** breakdown — each domain's share of total normalised score.

## 6. Response quality

v1 carried a single consistency check that compared, per theme, average
endorsement of positive statements against average endorsement of reverse ones.
It caught genuine self-contradiction. **It did not catch random responding**, and
a respondent clicking at random reliably received a "High" rating — confirmed in
live testing. Random answers average to the midpoint on both keyings, so nothing
crosses the contradiction threshold.

v2 replaces it with four independent signals.

### 6.1 Transitivity (primary)

If a respondent prefers A over B and B over C, they should prefer A over C.
Random responding produces **circular triads** at close to the chance rate;
a coherent respondent produces very few. The check needs no extra items — it is
computed from responses already collected.

Every item where one theme is favoured over another contributes a directed edge.
Triads in which all three pairings are present are examined, and the proportion
that form a cycle is the **circularity** index.

**Only pairings of two positively keyed statements contribute edges.** This is
essential, and was not obvious. When a reverse statement appears, the losing side
reflects *which statement was drawn* rather than the theme's standing: a
respondent strongly high on Driver who is shown "I often delay getting started"
against a positive Organiser statement will favour Organiser, even though Driver
is their stronger theme. The instrument's own design therefore manufactures
circular triads independently of how honestly the person answered. Computed over
all pairings, genuine and random respondents were nearly indistinguishable;
restricted to positive–positive pairings, they separate cleanly.

### 6.2 Profile spread

Random responding regresses every theme toward zero, because each theme wins and
loses in roughly equal measure. A differentiated profile spreads. The standard
deviation of raw theme scores captures this and is independent of transitivity.

### 6.3 Self-contradiction

Retained from v1. Counts themes where **both** the positive and the reverse
statements were strongly endorsed (average above 1.15 on each). This catches
acquiescence, which the transitivity check does not.

### 6.4 Position dominance and neutral rate

The largest share taken by any single scale position catches straight-lining —
always pressing the same key. The share of items left at the midpoint catches
disengagement and timeout-heavy sittings.

### 6.5 Calibration

Thresholds were set by simulation, 200 trials each of a genuine respondent
(stable latent preferences plus noise) and a uniform random responder:

| Signal | Genuine (5th–95th pct) | Random (5th–95th pct) |
|---|---|---|
| Circularity | 0.000 – 0.139 | 0.143 – 0.410 |
| Profile spread | 3.86 – 4.78 | 1.74 – 3.08 |
| Position dominance | 0.30 – 0.38 | 0.22 – 0.29 |
| Neutral rate | 0.08 – 0.16 | 0.14 – 0.26 |

Each signal contributes flags — two when badly out of range, one when marginal.
Three or more flags reads **Low**, one or two **Moderate**, none **High**.

Observed behaviour across 200 trials each:

| Behaviour | v1 rating | v2 rating |
|---|---|---|
| Uniform random clicking | High | Low (194), Moderate (6) |
| Cycling 1-2-3-4-5 | High | Low |
| Always the same key | High | Low |
| Everything left at neutral | High | Low |
| Genuine respondent | High | High (200/200) |

**These thresholds are calibrated against simulated respondents.** Real response
patterns may sit differently — in particular, a genuinely undifferentiated person
will look flatter than the model assumes. Revisit after 30–50 real completions,
and treat a Low rating as a prompt to check the responses rather than as a
verdict.

**What it still cannot do.** The quality check identifies responses that do not
cohere. It cannot detect someone answering carefully but dishonestly — a
consistent self-presentation produces a clean, transitive, well-spread profile
regardless of whether it is true.

## 7. Validity features

- **Reverse-keyed statements** — 3 per theme, so 52 of the 130 items involve one.
  Endorsing a reverse statement counts against its theme, so a theme can lose
  points. In a forced-choice format acquiescence is already suppressed; reverse
  items add an independent check and drive §6.3.
- **Response quality index** — see §6.

## 8. Psychometric caveats (read before using for assessment)

- **Ipsative data is intra-individual.** Scores describe strengths *relative to
  each other within one person*. Comparing a raw theme score across two people is
  statistically unsound — an inherent property of forced-choice measures, not a
  defect of this build. **Do not rank candidates by theme score.**
- **Not normed by design.** "High on X" means high *for you*, not high versus
  others.
- **Reliability ≠ validity.** Everything in §6 concerns whether the instrument
  measures *consistently*. Whether it measures *what it claims to* is a separate
  question requiring empirical study against external criteria — none has been
  run. The themes are face-valid and the structure is coherent; nothing here has
  been shown to predict any real-world outcome.
- **The first study worth running** is a factor analysis at a few hundred
  respondents, to check whether the 20 themes actually cluster into these 4
  domains. Everything before that is data collection.
- **Results are computed client-side** in the current build. For higher-stakes
  use, recompute and store scores server-side on submit.

## 9. Version history

**v2.0** — 12 themes → 20 (five per domain); 102 items → 130; statement bank 12 →
13 per theme so no statement repeats; appearances per theme 17 → 13; consistency
index replaced with the four-signal quality check in §6.

**Migration note.** Assessments completed under v1 reference themes that no
longer exist in the bank. Unlike a scoring-parameter change, this cannot be
resolved by recomputing from stored responses — the stored items are from a
different instrument. Either retain v1 as a versioned fallback for rendering old
reports, or treat the change as a clean break and accept that v1 reports become
unreadable.
