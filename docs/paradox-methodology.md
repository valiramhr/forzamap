# Paradox Profile — Methodology

Version 2.1 · 12 paradoxes · 24 traits · 120 items · 10-point Likert

*An original instrument for internal talent discussion. It borrows the general
idea of paradoxical trait pairing — a concept in psychology, not owned property —
but uses no content, traits, templates or scoring from Harrison Assessments or
any other proprietary instrument. It deliberately excludes job-fit scoring,
success formulas and population norming.*

---

## 1. Purpose and positioning

This instrument produces **conversation material for talent discussion**, not a
measurement of record. It plots a person's standing on twelve pairs of
complementary qualities and asks where they sit between them.

It is explicitly **not** a selection tool. There is no norming, no benchmark,
no fit score, and no pass mark. Reliability (§7) supports structured discussion;
it does not support decisions about individuals.

## 2. The paradox model

Each of the twelve paradoxes pairs a **dynamic** trait with a **gentle** trait.
The two are not opposites — they are complementary qualities that pull against
each other in day-to-day behaviour. Frankness and diplomacy are both good; the
interesting question is whether someone has both available to them.

| Paradox | Dynamic trait (y) | Gentle trait (x) |
|---|---|---|
| Influence | Assertiveness | Receptiveness |
| Communication | Frankness | Diplomacy |
| Decision-making | Decisiveness | Reflectiveness |
| Execution | Structure | Adaptability |
| Performance | Drive | Patience |
| Team contribution | Independence | Collaboration |
| Leadership | Direction | Empowerment |
| Standards | Accountability | Empathy |
| Risk | Courage | Prudence |
| Learning | Confidence | Curiosity |
| Problem-solving | Practicality | Creativity |
| Resilience | Persistence | Recovery |

## 3. Format

- **120 items**, five per trait: **3 positively keyed, 2 reverse keyed**.
- **10-point Likert**, 1 = strongly disagree to 10 = strongly agree.
- Untimed. Expect 10–14 minutes.
- Items presented in randomised order, mixed across traits, so respondents
  cannot see which items belong together.

Forced-choice was considered and rejected. Ipsative scoring tells you which of
two qualities a person prefers, but the paradox graph needs each trait's
**absolute** level independently — you must be able to see someone high on both.
Forced choice destroys exactly that information. The cost of Likert is
acquiescence and social-desirability bias, mitigated by reverse-keying (§5) and
flagged by the consistency index (§6).

## 4. Scoring

**Item score**
- Positively keyed: score = selected response
- Reverse keyed: score = 11 − selected response

**Trait score** = mean of that trait's five item scores. Range 1.0–10.0.

**Plot.** Each paradox renders as one 10×10 grid, dynamic trait on the vertical
axis, gentle trait on the horizontal. Quadrant lines at 5.5:

| Zone | Position | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Balanced | both ≥ 5.5 | both qualities available, applied situationally |
| One-sided (dynamic) | y ≥ 5.5, x < 5.5 | the dynamic quality without its counterweight |
| One-sided (gentle) | x ≥ 5.5, y < 5.5 | the gentle quality without its counterweight |
| Deficient | both < 5.5 | neither quality strongly present |

**Uncertainty.** Render each point with whiskers: on each axis, a bar spanning
±1 standard error of that trait's score, capped at both ends. The trait score is
a mean of five items, so the quantity to express is the uncertainty in that mean
— se = sd/√n over the items answered — not the item SD, which describes the
spread of the items themselves. Five items at ~0.7 reliability do not support
reading 6.2 against 5.8 as a real difference, and a bare dot implies precision
the instrument does not have.

Where an axis's standard error falls below 0.65 scale units, that axis's whisker
is omitted. In response terms the floor is an item SD of about 1.45 at five
items (0.65·√5) — items sitting roughly ±2 either side of the trait mean, which
is the trait's facets genuinely pulling apart. Below it the spread is nearer ±1,
the ordinary variation between five differently worded items on one trait, and a
whisker drawn there says "divided" about a trait that is not. The floor was
previously 0.35, set where the interval grew shorter than the dot drawn over it
— a fact about the mark rather than about the responses. The two axes are judged
independently, so a pair can carry a whisker on one trait and none on the other
— which correctly says one trait is settled and the other is not. Where both
fall below the floor the point renders alone.

**On the 5.5 threshold.** Both traits in every pair are socially desirable, so
against a fixed midpoint most respondents will cluster in the balanced quadrant
and the graph will under-discriminate. The lines are nevertheless fixed at 5.5,
on every panel and in every report: one grid, so that the twelve panels are
comparable with each other and two people's profiles are comparable at all.

*Person-centred lines* — quadrant boundaries at the individual's own mean across
all 24 traits — were tried and dropped. They remove social-desirability
inflation and suit an intra-individual instrument, but they force roughly half
of anyone's traits below the line by construction, they give each panel its own
grid, and they are not absolute measurement, so a reader comparing two
candidates would be comparing two different rulers.

*Local threshold* — once 50–100 people have completed the instrument, set the
boundary from your own respondent pool — remains the intended refinement, and
the scorer keeps the mode wired for it.

Until then, read "balanced" as *not evidently one-sided* rather than as an
achievement.

## 5. Reverse keying

Two of every trait's five items are reverse keyed. They serve two purposes:
they suppress acquiescence (agreeing with everything now costs you points), and
they supply the raw material for the consistency index.

**The rule every reverse item must follow:** it describes someone simply *low on
that trait*, and never invokes the paired quality. A reverse item for
Practicality that describes creativity does not measure absent practicality —
it measures present creativity with a minus sign, which forces the two traits
into negative correlation and collapses the pair onto a diagonal. Balanced and
deficient become unreachable and you have built an expensive bipolar scale.

Six original items violated this rule and were replaced in v2 (§12).

## 6. Consistency index

For each trait compute **P** (mean of its 3 positive items) and **R** (mean of
its 2 reverse items *after* the 11 − x conversion). Answered honestly these
converge in expectation, provided the two keying sets are facet-balanced.

| P − R gap | Reading |
|---|---|
| < 2.0 | consistent |
| 2.0 – 3.5 | mild |
| > 3.5 | flag this trait |

Across 24 traits: 0–3 flags = **High**, 4–7 = **Moderate**, 8+ = **Low**.

**Flag per trait, not only globally.** A flagged trait's average is a compromise
between two contradictory readings and represents neither. Render those points
hollow. Knowing which pair not to trust beats a single global warning.

**The facet caveat.** The gap separates contradiction from ordinary facet
variation only insofar as the positive and reverse sets tap comparable facets.
If the positives happen to sample facets a respondent is genuinely high on while
the reverses sample facets they are genuinely low on, the gap opens legitimately
and is indistinguishable from self-contradiction. This bank is well matched on
that count — each trait's reverse items mirror its positive content closely —
but it is a standing constraint on any new items.

**Do not use spread across all five items as the signal.** The five items tap
different facets; someone can legitimately rate one 9 and another 6 without
contradicting themselves.

### 6.1 The mild band is load-bearing

Only the > 3.5 flag drives the rating, but the 2.0–3.5 mild band carries real
information. Acquiescence at trait level T produces a gap of 2·|T − 5.5|, so a
respondent in the outer reaches of the blind band (§6.4) surfaces as mild even
though no flag fires.

Count mild traits and report the total — but interpret it against the
respondent's own noise level, not a fixed cut.

Estimate the two keying sets **separately**. Pooling assumes the positive and
reverse items are equally noisy; reverse-worded items reliably are not, and the
pooled error runs in the unsafe direction — it understates sd(P − R), making a
noisy respondent look more diagnostically usable than they are.

    σ̂ₚ  from positive-set deviations   (24 traits × 2 df = 48 df)
    σ̂ᵣ  from reverse-set deviations    (24 traits × 1 df = 24 df)
    sd(P − R) = √(σ̂ₚ²/3 + σ̂ᵣ²/2)

Take each trait's 3 positive item scores about their own mean, and its 2 reverse
scores about theirs. 24 df gives σ̂ᵣ a ~14% relative standard error — noisy but
adequate.

The mild count is only diagnostic at low noise:

| sd(P − R) | equivalent σ̂ | Per-trait mild rate | Expected mild traits | P(≥3 by noise) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.913 | 1.00 | 2.8% | 0.68 | 3% |
| 1.004 | 1.10 | 4.6% | 1.10 | 10% |
| 1.141 | 1.25 | 7.8% | 1.86 | 31% |
| 1.369 | 1.50 | 13.4% | 3.21 | 66% |

**Three or more mild traits signals mid-band acquiescence only when
sd(P − R) ≲ 1.0.** The ceiling is a judgement about tolerable noise rather than
a sharp break — at the ceiling itself the rule already carries a 10% false-alarm
rate, and by 1.37 three or more is the expected honest outcome. Report the
computed sd(P − R) alongside the count so the reading can be qualified.

**Cohort diagnostic on the bank.** σ̂ᵣ/σ̂ₚ **averaged over a cohort of 10 or
more** measures whether the reverse items are performing. The per-respondent
ratio carries ~18% relative standard error — a person whose true ratio is 1.0
will produce observed values from 0.70 to 1.42 in 95% of cases — so it means
nothing individually and must not appear on an individual report. Averaged, a
cohort ratio persistently above ~1.3 says the reverse items attract materially
more error than the positives and should be rewritten. That is the empirical
version of the §13 item review; 10 respondents separates 1.3 from 1.0 on
sampling error alone, more if between-person variation in the ratio proves
material.

### 6.2 The mid-scale blind band

For a respondent at trait level T who rates reverse statements with the same raw
number as positives — the acquiescence pattern — the gap is exactly

    gap = |2T − 11| = 2 × |T − 5.5|

Detectability is twice the distance from mid-scale. A threshold of G therefore
produces a blind band of ±G/2 around 5.5, width exactly G. At G = 3.5 that is
**T = 3.75 to 7.25**, 38.9% of the scale, within which acquiescence cannot be
flagged.

(This is the maximum for same-rating contradiction. If reverse answers are
arbitrary rather than matched, the achievable maximum is max(T−1, 10−T) ≥ 4.5
everywhere, so no level is unflaggable in principle. The band describes the
acquiescence pattern specifically, which is the realistic threat.)

At T = 5.5 this is an **identifiability failure, not a detection failure**. A
consistent mid-scale respondent answers 5.5 to positives and 5.5 raw to reverse
items, because 11 − 5.5 = 5.5. An acquiescent one answers 5.5 to everything. The
response vectors are identical, so no statistic over those five items can
separate them, and no threshold value fixes it.

The band is a structural property of a 2-item reverse subscale, not of the
threshold. The only lever that shrinks it is more reverse items: at 5 positive +
5 reverse, sd(P − R) falls from 0.913σ to 0.632σ and G = 2.0 becomes viable — at
the cost of a 240-item instrument. Not worth it here.

### 6.3 Why 3.5 and not lower

For a consistent respondent with per-item noise σ, sd(P − R) = 0.913σ. False
positives peak at mid-scale, precisely where true-positive power is lowest, and
fall to negligible at the extremes (~10⁻⁸ at G = 3.5).

| G | Blind band | % of scale | P(≥1 false flag) at sd(P − R) = 0.913 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.5 | ±1.75 | 38.9% | 0.30% |
| 3.0 | ±1.50 | 33.3% | 2.4% |
| 2.5 | ±1.25 | 27.8% | 13.7% |
| 2.0 | ±1.00 | 22.2% | 50.1% |

Tightening to 3.0 buys 5.6 percentage points of coverage for an eightfold rise
in false flags. 3.5 stands.

### 6.4 What actually survives every check

Acquiescence produces a gap of 2·|T − 5.5|, reaching 3.5 at the band edges and
therefore reading as *mild* across the outer portions:

| Acquiescent T | Gap | Reads as |
|---|---|---|
| 5.5 | 0.0 | consistent — genuinely identical |
| 6.0 / 5.0 | 1.0 | consistent |
| 6.5 / 4.5 | 2.0 | mild |
| 7.0 / 4.0 | 3.0 | mild |
| 7.25 / 3.75 | 3.5 | mild (band edge) |

The mild zones are [3.75, 4.5] and [6.5, 7.25] — 0.75 wide each — leaving an
invisible core of **T ∈ (4.5, 6.5)**, 22.2% of the scale rather than 38.9%. Only
T = 5.5 is irreducible, where §6.2's identifiability argument holds exactly;
detection shades off smoothly from there rather than switching on at the flag.

Narrowing that core is possible; closing it is not. More reverse items lower
sd(P − R) and so permit a lower mild threshold — at 5 positive + 5 reverse,
holding the per-trait false-positive rate constant, the threshold falls from 2.0
to 1.38 and the core narrows to 15.4%. Real, bounded, and not worth 240 items
(§6.2). Only out-of-band measures — near-duplicate items or response-time
capture — reach T = 5.5 itself, where the gap is identically zero at any item
count.

### 6.5 Assessable coverage

Report how many traits fall outside the blind band — where
|observed score − 5.5| > 1.75 — alongside the consistency rating.

The test is sound on *observed* scores because acquiescence compresses them.
Under full acquiescence on both reverse items the observed trait mean is
(3T + 2(11−T))/5 = (T + 22)/5, spanning only [4.6, 6.4] across the whole range
of T. A fully acquiescent respondent therefore cannot produce a verified extreme
score.

**Partial acquiescence leaks.** If only one reverse item is answered
acquiescently and the other honestly, then P = T, R = 5.5, giving
gap = |T − 5.5| and observed = (3T + 11)/5. For T in (8.42, 9] — and the mirror,
[2, 2.58) — the trait is neither flagged nor excluded from coverage. Both windows
are half-open: at T = 8.4167 the observed score is exactly 7.25 and the coverage
test is strict, so it does not leak; below T = 2 the flag fires (T = 1.9 gives a
gap of 3.6). The window is narrow and the score is still compressed (T = 8.8
reports as 7.5), so the distortion is bounded — but the guarantee holds only
under full acquiescence.

Coverage is usually low, because real profiles cluster:

| SD of a person's 24 trait scores | In blind band | Verifiable |
|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | 92% | 2 of 24 |
| 1.5 | 76% | 6 of 24 |
| 2.0 | 62% | 9 of 24 |
| 2.5 | 52% | 12 of 24 |

A typical profile sits at SD 1.5–2.0, so expect 6–9 verifiable traits. This is
why coverage must be reported alongside the rating: "High consistency, verified
on 7 of 24 traits" is a materially weaker claim than the rating alone implies.

### 6.6 Straight-lining

Compute the SD of all 120 raw responses. This is a **disengagement screen only**.

A genuine respondent's raw SD is at least √(s² + σ²) where s is between-trait
spread. Off mid-scale it rises further, because positive items cluster at μ while
reverse items cluster at 11−μ:

    raw SD = √(s² + σ² + 0.24·(2μ − 11)²)

At s = 1.5, σ = 1.0 that gives 1.80 at μ = 5.5, 2.05 at μ = 6.5, 2.33 at μ = 7.
The mid-scale figure is a floor, so the thresholds below stay conservative.

| Raw SD | Reading |
|---|---|
| ≤ 0.6 | straight-lining — treat the profile as invalid |
| 0.6 – 1.2 | indeterminate — cannot distinguish narrow-band acquiescence from a genuinely flat profile |
| > 1.2 | normal engagement |

**This check does not rescue §6.2.** A mid-scale acquiescent respondent using a
4–7 band has raw SD 1.118, and an honest flat profile at s = 1.0, σ = 0.7 has
1.22. The two overlap. Any cut high enough to catch the first invalidates some
of the second. The middle tier will therefore collect some honest profiles by
design — which is why it is labelled indeterminate rather than invalid.

### 6.7 Random responding

Under uniform random answers sd(P − R) = 2.622, giving an 18.2% flag rate per
trait and ~4.4 expected flags across 24 (sd 1.9). A pure random responder reads
**Moderate 63% of the time, High 32%, Low 5%**, with an expected gap of 2.09 —
in the mild band.

So the index has real but weak power here. Random responding is *under-detected
and usually indistinguishable from a genuinely mixed profile*, not invisible.
Catching it reliably requires near-duplicate items or an explicit infrequency
item. Neither is in v2.

### 6.8 Summary of detection power

| Failure mode | Detection |
|---|---|
| Acquiescence at extreme trait levels (T < 3.75 or > 7.25) | Reliably flagged |
| Acquiescence in the outer band ([3.75, 4.5], [6.5, 7.25]) | Surfaces as mild; usable when sd(P − R) ≲ 1.0 |
| Acquiescence in the core (4.5 < T < 6.5) | **Not detected** |
| Acquiescence at exactly T = 5.5 | **Not detectable in principle** |
| Random responding | Weak — Moderate rating 63% of the time |
| Total straight-lining | Caught by raw SD ≤ 0.6 |
| Narrow-band responding (e.g. 4–7) | Indeterminate; overlaps honest flat profiles |

Report the consistency rating, the flagged count, the mild count, the coverage
count, and sd(P − R) together. The rating alone overstates what is known.


## 7. Psychometric caveats

- **Reliability.** Five items per trait should give internal consistency around
  0.65–0.80. Adequate for structured discussion. Not adequate for selection,
  promotion decisions, or any use where the number carries consequences.
- **Not normed.** No population benchmark. A score of 7 means "7 on this scale",
  not "above average".
- **Self-report.** Measures self-perception, which correlates imperfectly with
  observed behaviour. Best used alongside manager and peer input, not instead
  of it.
- **Unvalidated.** No criterion or construct validity study has been run. The
  traits are face-valid and the structure is coherent, but nothing here has been
  demonstrated to predict any real-world outcome.
- **First-cohort review.** Several items (§13) are flagged as likely to
  under-discriminate. Review item-level distributions after the first 30–50
  completions and replace anything with a ceiling effect or near-zero variance.

## 8. Using the output

The graph is a prompt for conversation, not a verdict. Productive framing:

- Start with the pairs furthest from balance — those carry the most discussion.
- Ask what the one-sided position costs in practice, and where the person has
  seen it work.
- Treat deficient readings as "not currently a strength I draw on", not as a
  defect.
- Ignore flagged (hollow) points, or use them only to ask whether the questions
  read ambiguously.
- Never compare two people's scores on the same trait as though the difference
  were precise.

## 10. Online results page

**Grid.** 3 panels across on desktop (≥1024px), 2 on tablet, 1 on mobile, in the
order above.

**Each panel**: paradox name as heading; square 1–10 plot; y-axis label
(dynamic) above-left, not rotated; x-axis label (gentle) beneath; crosshair at
5.5 on both axes, identically on every panel and in every report; all four
quadrant labels in position; the point at (gentle, dynamic) with per-axis
uncertainty whiskers at ±1 standard error, each omitted where that axis falls
below the 0.65 floor; trait scores shown numerically.

**Legend.** Once above the grid, not per panel: plain dot, dot with whiskers,
the quadrant tint, and the hollow dot, each with its sample mark. Two lines.

**Emphasis.** Occupied quadrant filled with a light Forza tint, its label in Ink
at weight 500; the other three in Muted at 400 on Paper.

**Flagged pairs.** Where either trait's gap exceeds 3.5, render the point hollow
and caption the panel: "Responses on this pair were inconsistent — interpret
with care."

**Summary strip.** Consistency rating, flagged pair count and flagged trait count
(these differ — two flagged traits can share a paradox), mild count, coverage
count, sd(P − R), and zone counts.

from paper, not a screen preview.

## 13. Items flagged for first-cohort review

Not changed in v2, but watch these in real data.

**Mildly cross-loaded, retained.** Frankness R1 ("soften so much that the main
point can be lost"), Decisiveness R1 ("eliminate every uncertainty"),
Empowerment R1 ("closely controlled to prevent mistakes") and Patience R1
("frustrated when others cannot match my pace") each reference the paired
quality but carry a pathology marker — *so much that*, *every*, *closely*,
*frustrated* — which shifts them from describing the partner trait to describing
its excess. That is the correct pattern. Retained deliberately.

**Watch for weak discrimination.** Structure R1 ("detailed planning tends to
restrict rather than help me") and Diplomacy R1 ("when I am convinced I am
right, how I communicate matters less") are mildly cross-loaded without a
pathology marker. Drive R1 ("I am satisfied when expectations have been met") is
mild enough that high-drive respondents may still endorse it.

**Cross-trait overlap.** Courage P2 ("I raise difficult issues even when doing so
carries personal risk") is behaviourally close to Assertiveness P2 and Frankness
P3. Expect those three traits to correlate. If the redundancy proves
unhelpful, narrow Courage toward non-interpersonal risk.

**Weak paradoxes.** Confidence/Curiosity and Practicality/Creativity are not in
genuine tension the way Frankness/Diplomacy is — one can be curious *because*
one is confident. These pairs may produce less interesting quadrant reads than
the other ten.

## 14. Item bank

Key: `P` positively keyed · `R` reverse keyed (`R†` = replaced in v2).

### 1. Influence

**Assertiveness** — dynamic (y)
1. I express my position even when others may disagree. `P`
2. I am comfortable challenging a decision that I believe is flawed. `P`
3. I speak up early rather than waiting to see which way a discussion goes. `P`
4. I sometimes keep my views to myself to avoid resistance. `R`
5. I let others carry the discussion rather than putting my own view forward. `R`

**Receptiveness** — gentle (x)
1. I actively seek perspectives that differ from my own. `P`
2. I can change my mind when someone presents stronger reasoning. `P`
3. I ask people to explain their reasoning before I evaluate their conclusion. `P`
4. Once I have formed an opinion, further discussion rarely changes it. `R`
5. I tend to hear out other views without really weighing them. `R`

### 2. Communication

**Frankness** — dynamic (y)
1. I tell people clearly when their work does not meet expectations. `P`
